**TL;DR:** Flood damage cleanup in Tacoma typically costs $2,500 to $50,000 or more. The range depends on how much water came in, how contaminated it is, and how many rooms it reached. A flooded basement with clear groundwater sits on the low end. A whole-house flood with sewage contamination or structural drying needs sits much higher. Ask for a written scope before any work starts so the price is tied to a documented plan, not a guess.

If water from a storm, an overflowing creek, or a backed-up drain just came into your Tacoma home, you probably want a number before you decide who to call. South Tacoma and low-lying areas near the Puyallup River see this most often during Pacific Northwest atmospheric river events, when saturated ground pushes water into basements and crawlspaces faster than it can drain. The price swings more than people expect, and the biggest swing factor isn't square footage. It's what kind of water came in.

## How Much Does Flood Damage Cleanup Cost in Tacoma, WA?

Most Tacoma flood cleanup jobs run between $2,500 and $50,000, with the majority of residential basement and first-floor losses landing between $4,000 and $18,000. The spread is this wide because "flood cleanup" covers everything from a few inches of clean groundwater in a crawlspace to a whole first floor contaminated by sewage backup.

| Scenario | Typical Cost Range |
|---|---|
| Crawlspace or sub-area flooding, minimal materials affected | $1,500 - $4,500 |
| Basement flooding, clear groundwater intrusion (Category 2), limited drywall/flooring removal | $2,500 - $6,500 |
| Basement flooding with sewage or storm-surge contamination (Category 3), carpet and pad removed | $5,000 - $14,000 |
| First-floor flood intrusion, multiple rooms, 2-4 feet of drywall removed | $8,000 - $20,000 |
| Whole-house flood with structural drying, antimicrobial treatment, and reconstruction prep | $20,000 - $50,000+ |

These are typical industry ranges, not a quote. Every Tacoma property is different, and the only way to get an accurate number is a written scope from an on-site assessment.

## What Drives Flood Cleanup Cost Up or Down?

The water category matters more than the square footage. A room of clean Category 1 water costs far less to dry than the same room contaminated by sewage, because contaminated materials usually get removed instead of dried in place.

Flood water rarely stays clean. Groundwater seeping in under hydrostatic pressure is classified as Category 2 (significant contamination) under the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/). Storm surge, surface water flowing in from outside, rising river water, and any backup from a sewer line are Category 3, also called black water. That distinction drives the biggest cost difference in a flood job, because Category 3 work requires PPE, antimicrobial treatment, and more aggressive removal of porous materials like carpet, pad, and often the bottom few feet of drywall.

Other factors that move the number:

- **Square footage and ceiling height** affected, which sets how many air movers and dehumidifiers a crew needs to stage.
- **How long the water sat** before cleanup started. Mold can begin colonizing wet materials within 24 to 48 hours, so a flood discovered on day one costs less to remediate than one found a week later.
- **What materials got wet.** Hardwood, plaster, and concrete (IICRC Class 4 drying situations) take longer and cost more to dry than open-framed, unfinished basements.
- **Monitoring days.** Most jobs bill daily moisture monitoring until materials hit a dry standard, typically 3 to 7 days depending on the structure.
- **Contents.** Furniture, boxes, and belongings that need to be moved, inventoried, or sent to a contents restoration facility add a separate line item.

A flooded basement in a South Tacoma rambler with an unfinished slab and no stored belongings will almost always cost less than a first-floor flood in a Proctor District craftsman with original hardwood and finished walls, even if the square footage is similar.

## Does Flood Insurance or Homeowners Insurance Pay for This?

Standard homeowners insurance generally does not cover flood damage. Flood, meaning surface water, overflowing rivers, storm surge, or rising groundwater, is specifically excluded from most homeowners policies and requires a separate flood insurance policy through the [National Flood Insurance Program](https://www.floodsmart.gov/) or a private flood carrier.

This is the single biggest misunderstanding homeowners have after a flood. Homeowners insurance usually covers sudden, accidental water damage from inside the home, like a burst pipe. It usually does not cover water that enters from outside the structure, even during the same storm. Sewer or drain backup and sump pump failure are also excluded from most standard policies unless you've added a water backup endorsement.

If you carry an NFIP or private flood policy, document everything before cleanup starts: photos, a list of affected contents, and the water line height on walls. Washington homeowners who have questions about what a specific policy covers can check with the [Washington Office of the Insurance Commissioner](https://www.insurance.wa.gov/), which handles consumer complaints and coverage questions for policies sold in the state. ## How Do You Read a Flood Cleanup Estimate?

Most restoration estimates in Washington are built on Xactimate, the line-item pricing software insurance adjusters also use, so the two sides are working from the same reference numbers. Line items typically separate into a few buckets: water extraction (priced per square foot or per hundred square feet), equipment rental (air movers and dehumidifiers billed per unit per day), monitoring visits, antimicrobial application, demolition/tear-out of contaminated materials, and disposal.

A legitimate estimate names the water category and class for each affected area, because that's what justifies the line items that follow. If an estimate just lists a flat total with no category, class, or equipment count, ask for the breakdown. You're entitled to see why a $6,000 job isn't a $3,000 job.
