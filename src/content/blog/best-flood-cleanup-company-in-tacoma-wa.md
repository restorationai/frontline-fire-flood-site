**TL;DR:** The right flood cleanup company for a Tacoma home holds IICRC Certified Firm status, documents the job with a written drying plan and daily moisture log, and works directly with your insurance adjuster instead of leaving you to translate the claim on your own. Before you sign anything, get a written work authorization with a clear scope, ask whether the company classifies the water correctly (clean, gray, or black), and watch for storm chasers who show up door to door after a Pierce County storm with no local address and a blank form to sign.

If storm runoff backed up near Nalley Valley or a sump pump failed in a Swan Creek-area crawlspace and your floor is standing in an inch of water right now, you don't have time to interview five companies. You need a fast, reliable way to tell whether the crew pulling up to your curb is actually equipped to dry a flooded structure, or just a carpet-cleaning outfit with a couple of box fans. The checklist below is what to look for in the first phone call, before a single piece of equipment comes off the truck.

## What separates a flood cleanup company from a carpet cleaner?

A flood cleanup company extracts standing water, measures moisture inside walls and subfloors, and runs a structural drying plan until the building hits a verified dry standard. A carpet cleaner extracts surface water and cleans the top of the carpet; it does not test what's happening underneath or inside the drywall.

Flood water that has been sitting for more than a few hours often qualifies as Category 3, or black water, under the [IICRC's S500 water damage standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), which means porous materials like carpet pad, baseboard, and sometimes drywall need to come out rather than just be dried in place. A company equipped for flood work shows up with truck-mounted extraction, commercial dehumidifiers, and a moisture meter, not just a wet vac and a mop.

## Why does IICRC certification actually matter here?

IICRC certification means the technicians working in your home were trained to the industry's own water damage and structural drying standards, not just general cleaning. The Institute of Inspection, Cleaning and Restoration Certification sets the protocols the restoration industry uses nationwide, and you can confirm a firm's status directly on the [IICRC website](https://www.iicrc.org/).

Ask the company which IICRC credentials their technicians hold: a Water Damage Restoration Technician (WRT) or Applied Structural Drying (ASD) credential tells you someone on site actually knows how to read a psychrometric chart and set drying equipment correctly, not just place fans in a circle and hope.

## What questions should you ask before you sign anything?

Ask for a written scope of work and an itemized estimate before any demolition starts, not after. A legitimate company puts the plan on paper first.

Specifically, ask: What water category is this, and why? Will you provide daily moisture readings I can see? Do you bill my insurance directly or do I pay you and get reimbursed? Is there a cancellation clause if I want a second opinion? A company that answers these clearly, in writing, is behaving the way a licensed Washington contractor should. You can confirm any Washington contractor's license status through the state's [L&I contractor verification tool](https://secure.lni.wa.gov/verify/) before you sign.

## Does the company work directly with your insurance adjuster?

The best flood cleanup companies coordinate with your adjuster directly, submitting photos, moisture logs, and a documented scope so the claim moves without you translating technical findings yourself. That coordination matters because most standard homeowners policies cover sudden water damage from inside the home but exclude rising flood water and sewer or sump pump backups unless you've added separate flood or water-backup coverage.

If the flooding came from a river, storm surge, or surface water running in from outside, that typically falls under a separate flood insurance policy through the National Flood Insurance Program, not your standard homeowners policy. If you're unsure what your policy actually covers, Washington's [Office of the Insurance Commissioner](https://www.insurance.wa.gov/) publishes consumer guidance on flood and water-backup coverage gaps. A company that flags this distinction for you upfront, rather than assuming everything's covered, is doing you a real favor. ## What are the red flags of a storm chaser after a Pierce County flood?

A storm chaser typically shows up door to door right after a storm, has no permanent local address, and pressures you to sign a document before explaining what it authorizes. These are the companies that follow weather events from region to region rather than operating as an established part of the community.

Other red flags: demanding full payment upfront in cash, refusing to put a scope of work in writing, pushing you to sign an "assignment of benefits" form that hands your entire insurance claim over to them, or having no visible IICRC certification or state contractor license. If a crew can't answer basic questions about water categories or drying standards, that's a sign they're not equipped for flood-level water, regardless of what the side of their van says.

## What should a written drying plan and moisture log include?

A proper drying plan lists the water category and class of loss, the equipment deployed room by room, and a target dry standard based on readings from unaffected materials in your home. The moisture log then tracks progress against that target daily, not just at the start and end of the job.

At minimum, expect initial moisture readings for each affected material, a daily follow-up reading while equipment runs, photos documenting the affected areas, and a final readout confirming the structure reached a dry standard before equipment comes out. This paperwork is also what your insurance adjuster will want to see, so a company that keeps it organized from day one saves you a second round of questions later.

Tacoma's older housing stock, especially closer to the Tacoma Dome District and the crawlspace-heavy lots near Hilltop, tends to trap moisture in places a quick visual check won't catch. A flood cleanup crew that checks subfloor and wall-cavity moisture, not just the visible carpet, is the one equipped for this kind of building.
