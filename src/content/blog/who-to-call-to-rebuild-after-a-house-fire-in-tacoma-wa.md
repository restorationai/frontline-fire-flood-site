**TL;DR:** Once fire damage cleanup is done, rebuilding the structure, new framing, drywall, flooring, paint, is a separate phase called reconstruction. You can hire a general contractor for that phase alone, or use a restoration company that handles fire cleanup, contents, and reconstruction under one scope. Either way, get a written reconstruction estimate tied to the insurance adjuster's scope before work starts, and confirm the City of Tacoma has issued permits for any structural, electrical, or plumbing repairs.

If the mitigation crew just finished and you're standing in a house with exposed studs, a bare subfloor, and no drywall where the kitchen used to be, you've hit the point most Tacoma homeowners don't expect: cleanup is over, but the house still isn't livable. Homeowners in the North End and Stadium District neighborhoods, and out toward Point Defiance, run into this same gap. Soot and smoke odor are gone, the structure is dry, and now someone has to put the walls, floors, and finishes back. That's reconstruction, and it's a different job than the cleanup crew already did.

## Who Rebuilds a House After a Fire: a Restoration Company or a General Contractor?

Either one can legally do the rebuild, but the simpler path is a company that handles fire cleanup and reconstruction under one continuous scope of work. When one team does both, the reconstruction estimate is written against the same documentation used for the cleanup claim, which means fewer duplicate inspections and less time spent re-explaining the damage to a new contractor.

If you split the job, a separate general contractor for the rebuild isn't wrong, it's common, especially for larger losses. But that contractor will need the mitigation company's drying logs, demolition records, and any moisture readings to write an accurate scope, and someone has to hand those documents over. A company that already did both the [fire damage restoration](/services/fire-damage-restoration/) and the [reconstruction](/services/reconstruction/) keeps that information in one file.

This post picks up where cleanup leaves off. If you haven't made that first call yet, see [who to call for fire damage restoration in Tacoma, WA](/blog/who-to-call-after-a-house-fire-in-tacoma-wa/) for the first 24 hours.

## What Happens Between Fire Cleanup and Reconstruction?

There's usually a gap between the last day of demolition and the first day framing starts, and that gap is where projects stall. The mitigation crew removes charred materials, runs air scrubbers to clear smoke odor, and confirms the structure is dry; then someone has to write a reconstruction scope, get it priced, pull permits, and schedule trades.

When the same company does both phases, that gap shrinks because the reconstruction estimate is drafted from the demolition findings directly, instead of waiting for a new contractor to inspect the property from scratch. Hidden damage found during demo, charred framing behind a wall that looked fine from the outside, gets documented once and carried straight into the rebuild estimate rather than becoming a second round of paperwork.

## What Happens to Your Furniture and Belongings During the Rebuild?

Belongings typically get packed out and moved off-site before reconstruction begins, so they aren't exposed to construction dust, drywall debris, or further smoke odor absorption. A documented pack-out also protects the contents claim, since everything is inventoried and photographed before it leaves the house.

Once items are cleaned (smoke odor removal, soot cleaning, or laundering, depending on the material) they go into climate-controlled storage until the structure is ready for move-back. [Contents restoration and storage](/services/contents-restoration-storage/) is its own line item on most fire claims, separate from the structural rebuild, so ask whoever is managing your contents to keep that inventory list, you'll need it for the final settlement.

## How Does the Insurance Adjuster's Estimate Fit With the Rebuild Cost?

The adjuster's initial estimate usually reflects only the damage visible at the time of inspection, not what gets uncovered once demolition opens the walls. A reconstruction scope written after demo often needs a supplement, an additional estimate submitted to the insurer for damage the adjuster couldn't see at first.

This is normal and expected on fire claims, not a red flag. Keep every receipt, photo, and moisture reading from the cleanup phase; they're what support the supplement request. Washington homeowners can review how claims and insurer obligations generally work through the [Washington Office of the Insurance Commissioner](https://www.insurance.wa.gov/), including how to file a complaint if a claim stalls. If you want a sense of typical fire restoration cost ranges before you compare the adjuster's number against a contractor bid, see [how much fire damage restoration costs in Tacoma](/blog/fire-damage-restoration-cost-in-tacoma-wa/).

## Do You Need Permits for Fire Damage Repairs in Tacoma?

Most structural framing, electrical, and plumbing repairs after a fire require a permit from the City of Tacoma's permit services before the work is closed in behind drywall. Cosmetic repairs, paint, flooring replacement, non-structural drywall, generally don't.

An inspector typically needs to sign off on framing and rough electrical before insulation and drywall go back up, so permit timing affects the rebuild schedule more than people expect. A contractor familiar with Tacoma's permitting process pulls the permits as part of the job and schedules inspections around them, rather than leaving that step for the homeowner to chase down mid-project.

Once the structure is framed, inspected, and ready to close in, the rebuild moves fast, drywall, paint, flooring, and trim typically go quicker than the framing and rough-in phases that come before them. If you're at the point where cleanup is finished and you need a written reconstruction scope, that's the conversation to have next with whoever is managing the project.
