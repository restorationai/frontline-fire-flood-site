## What certification should a sewage cleanup company actually have?

Look for a company whose technicians hold the IICRC Water Restoration Technician (WRT) certification and whose crew follows the [ANSI/IICRC S500 Standard for Professional Water Damage Restoration](https://iicrc.org/s500/), the industry protocol that governs how Category 3 water is supposed to be handled.

Under that standard, sewage from a backed-up drain, a sewer line, or a toilet overflow beyond the trap is always Category 3, grossly contaminated water, regardless of how clean it looks. That classification isn't optional paperwork. It determines which materials get removed instead of dried, what PPE the crew wears, and what gets treated with antimicrobial before anyone closes the walls back up. A company that can't name their certifying body or explain S500 in plain terms probably isn't trained to the standard.

## What should you actually see when the crew shows up?

On a real Category 3 job, you should see full personal protective equipment, physical containment, and antimicrobial treatment, not just a shop vac and a mop.

That means Tyvek suits, gloves, and respiratory protection on every technician who enters the affected area, consistent with [OSHA](https://www.osha.gov/) guidance on bloodborne and biological hazard exposure. It means poly sheeting or containment barriers separating the contaminated zone from the rest of the house, so sewage-affected air and debris don't spread to unaffected rooms. Porous materials that touched Category 3 water (carpet pad, drywall, baseboard, sometimes subfloor) typically get removed rather than dried in place, because they can't be reliably decontaminated. Everything that stays gets treated with an EPA-registered antimicrobial before the space is closed back up.

If a crew walks in without gloves, skips containment, or starts drying carpet that was sitting in raw sewage, that's a sign to ask more questions before they continue.

## Will insurance cover the sewage cleanup, and will the company document it correctly?

Most standard homeowners policies do not automatically cover sewer backup or sump pump failure. That coverage usually requires a separate water backup (sewer/sump) endorsement added to your policy, something worth checking before you assume it's included (see [Triple-I's overview of what a standard homeowners policy covers](https://www.iii.org/article/what-is-covered-by-standard-homeowners-insurance)).

Because of that, documentation matters more on a sewage loss than almost any other restoration job. A good company photographs the affected area before any work starts, logs moisture readings, keeps a written inventory of what's removed, and provides a scope of work your adjuster can review line by line. If a company doesn't mention documentation at all during the first call, ask who's going to produce the paperwork your claim will need.
