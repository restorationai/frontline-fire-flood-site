## What can actually be saved after a flood, and what usually can't?

Hard, non-porous items like solid wood furniture, metal frames, and glass usually clean up well if they're dried and sanitized quickly. Porous items that sat in flood water, upholstered furniture, mattresses, particleboard, carpet padding, generally can't be restored to a safe standard and get documented, then discarded.

Water that enters a home from outside, a flooded creek, storm surge, or other surface water flowing in, is classified as Category 3 water under the [IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), the standard's most contaminated class. Groundwater that seeps in through a foundation under hydrostatic pressure is classified as Category 2 instead. That classification, not sentiment, drives most of the save-or-discard decisions. Photos, paper documents, and some electronics sit in a gray area: if they're pulled from the water fast and handled by someone experienced in document and electronics recovery, more survives than most homeowners expect.

## What should you photograph before anything is moved?

Photograph every affected room from several angles before a single item gets touched, including the waterline on the walls and the condition of each piece of furniture. That documentation is what protects your contents claim later.

Get close-up shots of high-value items (electronics, rugs, artwork, anything with a serial number) and anything sentimental you'd want on record even if it ends up discarded. Keep any receipts or appraisals you still have access to. A restoration crew will build a formal inventory once they arrive, but your own phone photos are the timestamp that shows the condition before cleanup began.

## How does a contents pack-out and inventory work?

A pack-out means a crew itemizes and removes your belongings room by room, photographing and cataloging each piece before it leaves the house. Salvageable items go to an offsite facility for cleaning; non-salvageable items are documented and set aside for your approval before disposal.

Cleaning methods vary by material: ultrasonic cleaning for hard goods, specialized processes for electronics and textiles, and document drying or digitization where it's warranted. The full inventory, with photos, gets shared with you and your adjuster so nothing is a surprise on the claim.

## Where do belongings go while the house dries and repairs happen?

Salvageable belongings typically move into climate-controlled storage until the structural drying and any rebuild work is finished at the house. That protects cleaned items from re-exposure to moisture while repairs are underway, and it clears the space so drying equipment and crews can work efficiently.

Items come back once the home passes final drying checks, usually delivered and placed back in their original rooms rather than left in boxes.

## How does this work with my insurance adjuster?

Contents damage is usually a separate line item from the structural (dwelling) claim, and it needs its own documentation to get paid fairly. The itemized, photographed inventory from the pack-out is what your adjuster will want to see.

One thing to know before you assume coverage: a standard homeowners policy usually does NOT cover flood water that enters from outside, storm surge, an overflowing creek, or rising groundwater, unless you carry a separate flood policy through the [National Flood Insurance Program](https://www.floodsmart.gov/) or a private flood insurer. If the water came from inside the house instead (a burst pipe, a failed appliance line), that's typically a different, more commonly covered scenario; see our [plain-English breakdown of what homeowners insurance covers](/blog/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-water-damage/) for the distinction. Washington's [Office of the Insurance Commissioner](https://www.insurance.wa.gov/) is a good resource if you're unsure what your specific policy includes.

A documented, professionally handled contents claim, separate inventories for salvaged items, discarded items, and cleaning invoices, tends to move faster through adjuster review than a homeowner's own list reconstructed from memory.
