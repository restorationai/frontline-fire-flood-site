If you just lifted the crawl space hatch in your Tacoma home and found standing water, the right call depends on where that water came from, not just the fact that it's there. For water from a failed pipe, fitting, or appliance line under the house, Frontline Fire & Flood is a Tacoma-area, IICRC Certified Firm that extracts standing water, sets structural drying equipment, and documents the loss for your insurance carrier, with crews taking calls 24/7. If water is still actively running from a plumbing source, shut it off at the fixture valve or the main first. No amount of drying equipment can keep up with a crawl space that's still filling.

## Why do crawl spaces flood in Tacoma homes?

Most Tacoma crawl space floods trace back to one of three sources: a burst or corroded supply line under the house, groundwater pushing up through a vented foundation during heavy fall and winter rain, or a sewer lateral backing up through the lowest drain in the house.

A lot of Tacoma's older housing stock, from the North End down toward the Tacoma Narrows, was built with vented crawl space foundations sitting close to grade. Puget Sound's clay-heavy soils hold water instead of draining it, so when a storm overwhelms a perimeter drain or downspout, that water has nowhere to go but under the house. New construction with sealed crawl spaces floods less often from rain, but is just as vulnerable to a failed supply line.

## Is it clean water, gray water, or sewage?

The source of the water determines the category of loss under the [ANSI/IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/), and that category decides what has to happen next.

A burst supply line starts as Category 1, or clean, water. The longer it sits in crawl space dirt and insulation, the more it picks up contaminants. Groundwater seeping in through the foundation under pressure is Category 2, gray water, even though it looks like plain rainwater. A sewer lateral backing up through a drain is Category 3, black water, and that's true no matter how diluted it looks. Never treat a sewer backup under the house as if it were clean water. If you smell sewage or see dark water pooling near a drain line, stay out of the crawl space and treat it as contaminated until a technician confirms otherwise.

## Plumber, drainage contractor, or water damage restoration: who do I call?

Who to call first depends on whether water is still coming in and where it's coming from, not on how much standing water you can see.

If a supply line or fixture is actively leaking, a plumber needs to stop that source first. Once the leak is stopped, the standing water and wet insulation still need to be pumped out and dried, which is where a restoration crew comes in for the [emergency water removal](/services/emergency-water-removal/) itself. If the flooding is from groundwater or storm runoff with no plumbing failure, a drainage contractor is the right call for the long-term fix (regrading, French drains, downspout extensions), but the water already under the house still needs extraction and drying before it sits long enough to cause rot or mold. If sewage is involved, this is not a DIY job. Our [sewage backup guide for Tacoma](/blog/who-to-call-for-a-sewage-backup-in-tacoma-wa/) covers that scenario in more depth. And if you see exposed wiring, an outlet, or a junction box sitting in or near the water, leave the crawl space and have an electrician or the utility check it before anyone goes back in.

## What happens when you call for a flooded crawl space?

A restoration crew starts with a few quick questions over the phone: how long the water has been there, whether the source is stopped, and whether there's any sewage smell.

On arrival, a technician confirms the source is off, then extracts standing water with a submersible pump or truck-mounted vacuum. They'll use a moisture meter to map how far the water traveled into subfloor insulation, framing, and ductwork, since crawl space damage is often wider than what's visible from the hatch. Air movers and a dehumidifier sized for the space go in next, following the same [water damage restoration](/services/water-damage-restoration/) drying protocol used for a basement or first floor. Photos and moisture readings get logged along the way, which matters when you file a claim.

## Does homeowners insurance cover a flooded crawl space?

Most standard homeowners policies cover sudden, accidental water damage, like a pipe that bursts without warning, but they exclude gradual leaks, flood water, and sewer backups unless you've added specific coverage.

A sudden supply line failure is typically covered. Groundwater or surface water seeping in is usually treated as flood damage, which requires a separate flood policy through the [National Flood Insurance Program](https://www.floodsmart.gov/) rather than your standard policy. A sewer backup is only covered if you carry a water backup, or sewer/sump, endorsement; without it, that type of loss is often denied. Our [plain-English guide to water damage and insurance](/blog/does-homeowners-insurance-cover-water-damage/) walks through what to document. Either way, taking photos before cleanup starts and keeping receipts protects your claim regardless of which category applies.

## What about mold risk under the house?

Mold can begin growing on wet wood and insulation within 24 to 48 hours, according to the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold), so a crawl space that's been damp for more than a couple of days is a real concern even if you can't see growth yet.

A musty smell coming up through floor vents or around the hatch is often the first sign, well before visible growth shows up. If the crawl space sat wet for more than two days, or if you can see fuzzy or discolored patches on the framing once it's dry, a [mold remediation](/services/mold-remediation/) assessment is worth getting before you close the hatch back up and forget about it.

If there's water under your Tacoma home right now, stop the source if it's safe to reach, then get the standing water out before it spreads further into the subfloor. A crawl space holds far less open air than a basement, so the same amount of water does more damage, faster, in a tighter space.
