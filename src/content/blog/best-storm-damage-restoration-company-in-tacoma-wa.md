## What does a storm damage restoration company actually do differently than a roofer?

A storm damage restoration company stabilizes the building and dries it out; a roofer replaces the roof covering. Those are two different trades, and a storm claim usually needs both, often in a specific order.

When wind or hail breaches a roof or siding, the first priority is stopping ongoing water intrusion: tarping the opening, extracting standing water, and starting structural drying before mold has a chance to start (mold can begin growing on wet materials within 24 to 48 hours, per the [EPA](https://www.epa.gov/mold)). That's mitigation work, governed by the [ANSI/IICRC S500 water damage standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/). Only after the structure is stabilized does the permanent roof repair happen, which is roofing trade work.

Some roofing contractors advertise "storm damage restoration" but only handle the shingles. If a company can't describe how they'll dry out a soaked attic or document the interior loss for your adjuster, they're a roofer, not a restoration company. ## What should you check before hiring a storm damage company in Tacoma?

Check four things: the Washington contractor license, IICRC certification, how they document for insurance, and how fast they can actually get a tarp on the property. Anyone can claim all four on a phone call; only some can prove it.

- **License status.** Washington requires a registered contractor license for this work. You can verify any company's license status directly through the [Washington State Department of Labor & Industries contractor lookup](https://secure.lni.wa.gov/verify/). Frontline Fire & Flood is licensed under CCFRONTFF761CB; look up any company before they set foot on your property.
- **IICRC certification.** The [Institute of Inspection, Cleaning and Restoration Certification](https://www.iicrc.org/) trains and certifies technicians in water damage (WRT), structural drying (ASD), and fire/smoke restoration (FSRT). These aren't marketing badges; they're the standards an insurance adjuster will expect the work to follow.
  - **Insurance documentation.** Storm claims live or die on documentation: moisture readings, photos before tarping, a written scope of work. A company that skips this step is making your claim harder to win.
  - **Actual response capability.** "24/7" only means something if the company's hours actually say so. Ask directly: who shows up, how fast, and what do they do first.

## What questions should you ask a storm damage contractor before signing anything?

Ask these four before you sign a work authorization: are you licensed in Washington, are you IICRC certified, will you provide a written scope before billing, and will you document the loss for my insurance company. A company that hedges on any of these is worth a second call.

Other questions worth asking on that first phone call: What water category are we dealing with? (Wind-driven rain and water that's come in from outside the envelope is often treated as more contaminated than a simple roof drip, which starts clean but degrades the longer it sits, per the [IICRC S500 standard](https://iicrc.org/s500/).) Will you tarp or board up today, or just give me an estimate? Do you subcontract the drying work, or is it your own crew? What's included in a written scope versus a verbal estimate?

If the person on the phone can't answer the IICRC or licensing question without checking, that's useful information too.

## Will homeowners insurance cover storm damage repair?

Most homeowners policies cover sudden, wind-related storm damage, like a tree limb through the roof or shingles torn off in a windstorm, but they draw a hard line at flood water. Check your specific policy, since coverage details vary by insurer.

Flood damage, meaning rising surface water from outside the home, typically requires a separate flood policy and isn't covered under a standard homeowners policy, per the [Insurance Information Institute](https://www.iii.org/article/which-disasters-are-covered-by-homeowners-insurance). Wind and rain intrusion through a storm-damaged roof is usually treated differently than groundwater flooding, so it matters which one actually happened at your property. If you have questions about your specific policy language, the [Washington Office of the Insurance Commissioner](https://www.insurance.wa.gov/) is the state resource for homeowner insurance questions.
